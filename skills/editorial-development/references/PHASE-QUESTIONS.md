# Diagnostic Questions by Phase

A comprehensive reference for editorial inquiry at each phase. Use during manuscript assessment, author conversations, or when stuck on what to look for in a given section. These expand on the diagnostic questions embedded in each phase of the main skill.

---

## Phase 1: Concept

**About the book:**
- What is this book fundamentally about, in one sentence?
- What is the central concept — not the subject, but the angle or claim?
- Is the concept big enough to sustain a book? Too big to handle in one volume?
- What problem does this book solve for the reader, or what experience does it offer?
- What would be lost from the reader's life if this book did not exist?

**About the audience:**
- Who is the primary reader? (Resist "general readers" — describe a specific person.)
- What does that reader already know about this subject?
- What do they want from a book about it?
- Who is the secondary reader, and how do their needs differ?
- Are there audiences the book should not try to serve?

**About the list and competition:**
- Where does this book sit on the publisher's list? What does it need to accomplish to justify its position?
- What are the three most direct competitors? How is this book different?
- Is the book positioned correctly, or is it trying to be several different books at once?

---

## Phase 2: Content

**About what's there:**
- What is the richest, most original material in this manuscript?
- What material is present out of habit, convention, or duty rather than necessity?
- What is missing that the argument requires?
- What is present that the argument doesn't use?

**About the center:**
- Which concept, if placed at the center, would make the rest of the material cohere?
- Is the center currently visible, or is it buried?
- Is the author aware of what the center actually is?

**For first-time authors:**
- Is the author burying their best material because they distrust it?
- Are important claims relegated to footnotes or hedges?
- Does the author over-explain their methodological choices instead of using them?
- Is there a tension between "the book I was trained to write" and "the book this material is trying to become"?

**About the content mix:**
- Is the balance between evidence and argument right for the intended reader?
- Too many examples? Not enough? Wrong kind?
- Is the material too technical for the intended reader? Too basic?
- Are case studies pulling their weight, or are some there for texture rather than argument?

---

## Phase 3: Thesis

**Finding it:**
- If you had to reduce this book to one arguable claim — what is it?
- Could a reasonable person disagree with it?
- Is it specific? (Not "leadership matters" but "the most effective leaders in this study consistently did X, even when conventional wisdom said Y.")
- Does it appear clearly, early in the manuscript?

**Testing it:**
- Does each chapter advance, complicate, or demonstrate the thesis?
- Is there a chapter that doesn't connect to the thesis? What is it doing?
- Does the conclusion return to the thesis and advance it, or just restate it?
- Can you trace a continuous logical thread from the introduction thesis to the conclusion?

**The gap:**
- Is there a gap between what the author thinks the thesis is and what the manuscript actually argues?
- Has the author written a more interesting book than they think they've written?
- Is the real thesis hidden in a passage the author treats as a subordinate point?
- What is the author most excited to say? Is that excitement in the thesis?

**For the working title:**
- Does the title make a claim or promise an experience?
- Does it deliver on the book's actual argument?
- Is it searchable? Memorable? Does it work as a spoken recommendation?
- Does the subtitle do necessary work, or is it redundant?

---

## Phase 4: Narrative

**About the timeline choice:**
- Does the narrative strategy serve the thesis, or is it a default?
- Why this strategy and not another? Is there a reason to do it this way, or just a habit?
- What does the chosen strategy cost the reader? Is that cost worth it?
- If the author inherited the structure from a dissertation, article, or prior draft — does it still serve the material?

**About orientation:**
- At what point does the reader understand the stakes?
- Is that point in the right place?
- If nonlinear: what is the anchor that keeps the reader oriented?
- If linear: what is the propulsive question driving the reader forward?

**About the ending:**
- Does the narrative structure support the ending the author has written?
- Does the ending feel earned, or does the structure not lead there?
- What would have to change structurally for the ending to land as intended?

**About POV:**
- Is the POV choice consistent throughout?
- Where does the author intrude in a way that doesn't serve the material?
- Where does the author retreat from the material when their presence would strengthen it?

---

## Phase 5: Exposition

**About the spine:**
- Can you state what each chapter argues in one sentence?
- If not: is the expository spine missing, or just buried?
- Does each chapter's argument connect explicitly to the book-level thesis?
- Is there a chapter that makes a point the book never uses?

**About balance:**
- Where does exposition disappear and the chapter become a series of examples without connective argument?
- Where does exposition crowd out evidence — the author asserting without showing?
- Is the author's voice present in the exposition, or has it gone neutral and impersonal?
- Are there long stretches where the reader loses track of what the author thinks?

**About development:**
- Does each expository claim lead to the next, or do they sit side by side without connection?
- Does the book-level argument develop across chapters, or does each chapter make the same point with different evidence?
- At what point in the book does the argument reach its most complex or highest-stakes claim? Is that placement right?

---

## Phase 6: Plan

**Before writing the report:**
- Have you identified the single most important structural issue? Is it clearly named in the report?
- Is the report prioritized? (Structure before argument before voice.)
- Is every recommendation specific enough to act on?
- Have you read the report aloud? Does any sentence sting, or is any sentence so hedged it won't land?

**For author communication:**
- Have you shown that you understand why the author made the choices they made?
- Have you offered options, not mandates, on contested issues?
- Is the revision scope realistic given the author's time, contract, and experience level?
- What is the most important thing the author needs to hear? Have you said it clearly?

**After sending the report:**
- Has the author agreed on the revision direction before you proceed to line-level work?
- Are there issues where the author and editor genuinely disagree? How will those be resolved?

---

## Phase 7: Rhythm

**About pacing:**
- Does the book move at a consistent speed, or does it vary appropriately?
- Where does the reader slow down unintentionally? Where do they rush when they should slow down?
- Is the book spending page time in proportion to the importance of each idea?

**About structure:**
- Are section breaks in the right places?
- Do chapter openings set the right speed?
- Do chapter closings signal release, or do they just stop?
- Is there a chapter that feels like it's running at the wrong speed throughout — either rushed or labored?

**About individual passages:**
- Are there passages where the author is writing slowly through material they understand well because it feels important to them, but the reader will find it slow?
- Are there passages where the author rushes through genuinely difficult material because they're bored with it?

---

## Phase 8: Transitions

**Finding the gaps:**
- Can you follow the argument from paragraph to paragraph without rereading?
- Where do you lose the thread?
- Is the problem a missing transition or a missing argument?
- Are there places where a transition has been patched in over a logical discontinuity?

**Diagnosing the gap type:**
- Logical gap? (Argument skips a step the reader needs.)
- Tonal gap? (Register shifts without signal.)
- Temporal gap? (Time jumps without orientation.)
- Spatial gap? (Scene shifts without location.)
- Conceptual gap? (New term or framework appears without introduction.)

**About stitched manuscripts:**
- Are the seams structural (missing argument) or just stylistic (missing connecting prose)?
- Has the author tried to cover a logical discontinuity with a transitional paragraph?
- Do the chapter opening paragraphs do their job, or do they assume the reader has already oriented?

---

## Phase 9: Style

**About voice:**
- Is the author's voice consistent, or does it drift?
- What are the author's characteristic strengths? Where are they present? Where absent?
- What are the author's characteristic weaknesses? Are they patterns or one-offs?
- Where does the author's voice feel most alive? What is happening in those passages?

**About tone:**
- Which tone lapses appear in this manuscript? (See TONE-LAPSES.md for the full list.)
- Are the lapses concentrated in particular chapters or sections?
- Do the lapses have a common cause? (Academic training? Anxiety about the audience? Padding? Advocacy background?)

**About rhetoric:**
- What rhetorical gestures does the author use instinctively?
- Which gestures are completely absent from their range?
- Where are the flattest passages? Which gesture would animate them?
- Are there dead metaphors or clichéd analogies that have stopped doing work?

---

## Phase 10: Display

**About the system:**
- Does the heading system follow consistent logic and grammar?
- Do chapter headings make claims, ask questions, or set atmosphere — or are they purely descriptive?
- Is displayed matter integrated into the argument, or is it decorative?
- Does the heading series read as a coherent outline when scanned?

**About individual elements:**
- Does each sidebar pass the test: does it add something the running prose cannot provide efficiently?
- Are captions interpretive, not merely descriptive?
- Will charts and tables be legible at print size?
- Are epigraphs earning their place? Do they do more than just look literary?

**About the display narrative:**
- If you flip through looking only at displayed matter, does it tell a coherent secondary story?
- Does a reader who only reads headings and looks at images come away with the book's essential argument?
- Is there displayed matter that contradicts or complicates the running text's argument in ways that aren't intended?
