# Editorial Report Template

Use this template for Phase 6: Plan. Adapt length and depth to the scope of the engagement (proposal, sample chapters, or full manuscript). The template is a starting structure — reorganize sections as the specific manuscript requires.

### Overview

[2–4 paragraphs. Open with what the book is doing well and why it matters — be specific, not generic. Then name the central opportunity: the one change that would most significantly improve the book. Close with the editorial approach you're recommending and the spirit in which you're offering it.]

**How to write this paragraph:** Write it last. Name something specific the manuscript is doing well (not "the research is impressive" but "the case study in Chapter 3 is the kind of material that makes a book"). Then name the central opportunity as a possibility, not a problem: "The central opportunity is to let that material take center stage — which requires one structural move." Do not try to summarize all your notes here; do try to set the entire relationship.

### Structural Issues

[Work through the major structural issues in order of importance. For each issue, follow this format:]

**[Issue name]:** [1–2 sentences stating the problem clearly.] [1–3 sentences proposing a specific solution.] [1 sentence naming what this accomplishes.]

**Example:**
**Chapter sequence:** Chapters 4 through 6 cover overlapping territory, with the material on X appearing in all three without significant development between occurrences. Consider consolidating this material into Chapter 4, cutting the current Chapter 6, and allowing the space recovered to develop the underserved argument about Y (currently buried in a footnote on page 87). This would give the book's second half a clear forward trajectory it currently lacks.

## Template Notes

**On length:** A proposal report might be 3–5 pages. A full manuscript report is typically 8–15 pages. Longer is not better. Every sentence in the report should either name a specific problem or propose a specific solution.

**On tone:** The report is the author's first experience of you as a partner. Read it aloud before sending. If any sentence would sting if said face to face, rewrite it. If any sentence is so hedged it won't land, strengthen it.

**On prioritization:** Authors can only hold so much at once. If you have identified 20 issues, the report should name the 5 that matter most. The rest can wait for a follow-up call or the next revision round.

**On the overview paragraph:** Write it last. It's the hardest part and sets the entire relationship. A weak overview makes everything that follows harder for the author to receive.

**On chapter-by-chapter notes:** Resist the urge to document everything you noticed. The author will disengage if every chapter gets a dense list. Lead with the most important thing, then add 2–4 secondary notes. If you have more, save them for a follow-up conversation.

**On what not to include:** Do not include copyediting catches (spelling, grammar, punctuation) in the developmental report unless they're symptomatic of a style problem. That's a different pass. Do not include personal reactions ("I loved this part") without tying them to the editorial point they illustrate.
